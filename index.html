Thermal Detector

Goal
Build a device that detects changes in temperature and alerts the user, with a buzzer and a blinking LED, when the temperature moves below or above 70–75 °F. The project combined mechanical parts designed in Onshape, an Arduino circuit I built and analyzed, and a custom 3D-printed battery holder.
Tools
  • TinkerCAD
  • Onshape
  • Arduino IDE
  • Soldering
  • 3D printing
Timeline
Sep 2025 – Dec 2025

Results

Alert range70–75°F
SensorTMP36
Power9V

The design objectives were met to a great extent: the device is lightweight, meets the task requirements, and is wired properly.

The sensor calculates the temperature from an analog reading of the TMP36 and shows it accurately on the LCD.

Despite its limitations, the device is useful for monitoring temperature in a workshop or another controlled environment.

How it works

The TMP36 outputs a voltage proportional to temperature. The Arduino reads that voltage on an analog pin as a number from 0 to 1023, converts it back to millivolts, and then to degrees:

Voltage (mV) = (analogRead × Vref1023) × 1000

The TMP36 gives 10 mV per °C with a 500 mV offset, so °C = (mV − 500) / 10 and °F = °C × 9/5 + 32. The LCD shows the reading, and when it leaves 70–75 °F a buzzer sounds and an LED blinks.

Constraints and limitations

  • The 9 V battery gives a short operating time.
  • The device is fragile, so it is not ideal for daily use.
  • It works best in a controlled environment such as a workshop.

Launch Lug Fixture

Goal
Design the fixture that connects the launch lugs to Argo, BURPG’s main rocket, so the rocket hangs perfectly vertical for static testing, and check its strength before machining.
Tools
  • Fusion Design
  • Fusion CAM
  • CNC Mill
  • Manual Mill
Timeline
Sep 2026 – Oct 2026

Results

FOS, shear at LL2.54
FOS, shear at FB3.43
Critical width0.420in

Modeled the fixture, placed it in the full Argo airframe assembly, then machined it from aluminum and test-fitted it in its slot.

Why it matters

The rocket has to hang perfectly vertical during static testing, and the launch lugs are what align it. The lug is not load-bearing, so neither is the fixture, but I still calculated bolt preload and shear stress to find its factor of safety.

Design

The same two screw sizes join the launch lugs (LL) to the fixture and the fixture to the fin bar (FB).

Chosen screws
ScrewPreload (lbf)Torque (lbf·in)
6-321030.2715.32
4-40684.1228.435

My main concern was buckling, because the geometry is thin. In the end, contact between the rocket’s outer shells and the launch lug provides enough friction to reduce the stress in the bolts. That made the fixture’s width critical: it had to equal the thickness of the thrust structure’s outer shells plus the distance from the floor of the fin bar to where it meets the lug, 0.420 in ± 0.001.

Conclusion

The design is close to the safe range. Switching from aluminum to a material with higher yield and tensile strength would raise the factor of safety, since the fixture can’t be made any wider.

Biped Robot for Bits and Bolts

Goal
Give students with no previous hardware or software experience insight into electronic components and a chance to work with hardware. Because this was a collaboration with Girls Who Code, the workshop was also tailored to computer science majors. It was built for Bits and Bolts, an event by Girls Who Code and SHPE (the Society of Hispanic Professional Engineers) held for 30+ students.

Results

Pawn on the CNC lathe

Turned a chess pawn on the ProtoTRAK RLX lathe for training.

20-well plate for the PANDA BEAR lab

I milled a 20-well plate from PTFE, along with a matching 6061 aluminum mold fitting, for Polymer Analysis aNd Discovery Array - Bayesian Experimentation for Autonomous Research (PANDA-BEAR), a modular self-driving lab that measures drop angles. Both parts had to be machined on both sides. I generated the toolpaths in Fusion CAM, choosing the rpm carefully to keep machining time down, and checked the G-code before each run. I am now machining two more well-plate sets to update PANDA's hardware & software at Werner lab to the newest version PANDA-BEAR located at Professor Keith Brown's KABLab.

CAM and block model projects from class, plus an engineering drawing made in SolidWorks for one of the assignments.
